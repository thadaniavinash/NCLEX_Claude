## NURS 1017 Unit 7 Case Study 7

64-year-old female client at a community health clinic who reports a resolved episode of right-hand weakness and jumbled speech: recognizing a TIA, modifiable and non-modifiable stroke risk factors, same-day emergency referral, FAST teaching, anticoagulant and antihypertensive teaching, and evaluation of secondary prevention.

**Notes for review:**

- Nursing-focused case (assessment, priorities, teaching, evaluation), following OpenStax 15.2 for TIA and stroke prevention.
- Referral pathway follows the Canadian Stroke Best Practice Recommendations (same-day emergency assessment for a TIA with weakness or speech symptoms within 48 hours).
- Clinician review: the missed-dose rule for apixaban and the BP target after TIA.

**Rationale**

**Require follow-up:**

**Sudden one-sided hand weakness and numbness with jumbled speech that cleared in 15 minutes:** these are stroke warning signs. Even though they have gone, the nurse treats them as a possible transient ischemic attack (TIA) and records when they started and stopped (last known well: before 0730).

**Irregular pulse:** may be atrial fibrillation, a common source of clots that travel to the brain; the nurse counts the apical pulse for a full minute and reports it.

**BP 172/96 mm Hg after stopping amlodipine:** uncontrolled hypertension is the most important modifiable stroke risk factor. Stopping a medication because of a side effect without telling the provider is a teaching need.

**Smoking:** a modifiable risk factor that damages blood vessels and makes blood clot more easily.

**Do not require follow-up now:** her normal neurological assessment today (alert, clear speech, symmetrical smile, equal grips), temperature, respirations, oxygen saturation and capillary glucose. A normal glucose also rules out hypoglycemia as the cause of the episode.

- Smoking 1 pack per day → **Modifiable**

- Type 2 diabetes with an HbA1c of 8.6% → **Modifiable**
- A previous TIA (today's episode) → **Non-modifiable**

**Rationale**

**Why risk factors matter after a TIA:** a TIA is a temporary blockage of blood flow to part of the brain; the clot clears on its own, so the symptoms resolve without permanent damage. It is a warning: the risk of a stroke in the next 90 days is high (about 18%), and it is greatest in the first 48 hours. Teaching aims at the factors the client can change.

**Modifiable** (can be treated or changed): hypertension, smoking, physical inactivity, diabetes (glucose control), obesity, unhealthy diet, high cholesterol, heavy alcohol use, sleep apnea, and atrial fibrillation (treated with anticoagulation and rate control).

**Non-modifiable:** age, family history, sex, race and ethnicity, and a previous stroke or TIA. These cannot be changed, but they raise the client's overall risk, so the modifiable factors matter even more.

## NURS 1017 Unit 7 Case Study 8

34-year-old female client with a relapse of multiple sclerosis: focused neurological assessment, falls, urinary retention and infection risk, fatigue and heat sensitivity, nursing priorities and an interprofessional plan, mood and safety during high-dose corticosteroids, and discharge teaching for self-catheterization, energy conservation and ongoing therapy.

**Notes for review:**

- Nursing-focused case following OpenStax 17.3 (nursing care of the patient with MS); pathophysiology kept to the rationales.
- Different from Unit 7 Stand-alones 2 and 3 (MS teaching evaluation, ataxia care planning): heat and energy conservation appear again but in new situations.
- Clinician review: methylprednisolone order, post-void residual threshold (300 mL) and self-catheterization interval.

### Screen 1: Recognize (select_all)

The nurse reviews the client's history and assessment. Which findings require follow-up? **Select all that apply.**

1. Temperature 36.9° C
2. Numbness of the fingertips, unchanged for 2 years
3. **✓** Needs help to walk to the bathroom because of weak, stiff legs and an unsteady gait
4. **✓** Weakness is much worse after a hot shower
5. Wears glasses for reading
6. **✓** Bladder scan of 350 mL after voiding
7. **✓** Limits her fluid intake to about 800 mL a day
8. **✓** Double vision when looking to the left
9. **✓** States she has stopped seeing friends and feels like a burden

**Rationale**

**Require follow-up:** new leg weakness, stiffness (spasticity), an unsteady gait and double vision put her at high risk of falling. A post-void residual of 350 mL means her bladder is not emptying (urinary retention), and limiting fluids to avoid accidents concentrates the urine; both raise the risk of a urinary tract infection. Withdrawing from friends and feeling like a burden can signal depression, which is common in MS and needs screening and support. Worsening weakness after a hot shower (heat sensitivity) is important for safety and teaching.

**Do not require follow-up now:** fingertip numbness that has not changed in 2 years is her baseline (the nurse documents it to compare with later). A normal temperature is expected (and reassuring, because infection and fever can worsen MS symptoms). Reading glasses are unrelated.

**Why MS causes these findings:** MS is an autoimmune disease in which immune cells attack the myelin of the brain, optic nerves and spinal cord. Inflamed, demyelinated areas (plaques) slow or block nerve conduction, so symptoms depend on where the plaques are: motor weakness and spasticity, double vision, bladder dysfunction, sensory changes and fatigue. In a relapse, new inflammation causes new or worse symptoms over days; they often improve as inflammation settles (remission).

- Weakness worse after a hot shower → **Risk for falls, Fatigue**
- Needs to rest after dressing → **Fatigue**

**Rationale**

**Risk for falls:** weakness, spasticity and poor balance, double vision (impaired depth perception), hurried trips to the bathroom at night in the dark, and the temporary weakness that follows heat exposure.

**Risk for urinary tract infection:** urine left in the bladder (retention) lets bacteria multiply, and a low fluid intake reduces flushing of the urinary tract. A urinary tract infection, or any fever, can temporarily worsen MS symptoms (a pseudo-relapse), so prevention matters.

**Fatigue:** the most common MS symptom; demyelinated nerves need more energy to conduct impulses. Broken sleep from nocturia and heat make it worse. Heat slows conduction in demyelinated nerves even more, so symptoms worsen temporarily after a hot shower, exercise in the heat, or a fever (and improve with cooling).

## NURS 1017 Unit 7 Case Study 9

20-year-old university student with epilepsy admitted after a breakthrough tonic-clonic seizure: seizure triggers (missed medication, sleep loss, alcohol, missed meals), describing and classifying seizures from witness accounts, seizure precautions, care during and after a seizure (bow-tie), documentation, and self-management teaching.

**Notes for review:**

- Nursing-focused case following OpenStax 17.5 (nursing care of the patient with seizures).
- Screen 5 is a bow-tie (condition, 2 actions, 2 parameters).
- Clinician review: lorazepam order, missed-dose advice for levetiracetam, wording on provincial driving rules.

### Screen 1: Recognize (highlight)

The nurse reviews the Nurses' Notes. **Highlight** the findings that may have contributed to the client's seizure.

- [Nurses' Notes] **✓** ran out of levetiracetam 3 days ago and has not had time to refill it
- [Nurses' Notes] **✓** studying for final exams, sleeping about 4 hours a night for the past week
- [Nurses' Notes] **✓** Drank 5 beers at a friend's party last night
- [Nurses' Notes] **✓** Skipped breakfast and lunch today
- [Nurses' Notes] Had a coffee before going to the library
- [Nurses' Notes] ordinary overhead lighting
- [Nurses' Notes] Exercises at the campus gym 3 times a week
- [Nurses' Notes] sore tongue with a small bite on the right side

**Rationale**

**Possible contributors (triggers):**

**Missed antiseizure medication:** the most common cause of a breakthrough seizure. Levetiracetam levels fall within a day or two of stopping it.

**Sleep deprivation:** one of the strongest seizure triggers; exam periods are a common time for breakthrough seizures in students.

**Alcohol:** heavy drinking, and the withdrawal period the next day, lower the seizure threshold.

**Skipped meals:** low blood glucose and missed routines can trigger seizures.

**Not contributors here:** a usual amount of coffee, ordinary lighting (flashing or flickering lights affect only people with photosensitive epilepsy) and regular exercise. The tongue bite is a result of the seizure, not a cause.

**What a seizure is:** a sudden, abnormal, excessive electrical discharge of a group of neurons that changes movement, sensation, awareness or behaviour. Epilepsy is a tendency to have recurrent unprovoked seizures.

### Screen 2: Analyze (matrix_mc)

The client's roommate has witnessed several of her seizures and describes them to the nurse. For each description, click to specify the type of seizure it is most consistent with.

- “She gets a rising feeling in her stomach and smells burning rubber, but she can still talk to me.” → **Focal aware**
- “She stiffens, falls, and her arms and legs jerk; afterwards she's confused and sleeps for an hour.” → **Generalized tonic-clonic**
- “She stares, smacks her lips, and fiddles with her sleeve; she doesn't answer me and doesn't remember it later.” → **Focal impaired awareness**
- “When she was a child, she would stare blankly for a few seconds many times a day, then carry on as if nothing happened.” → **Absence**
- “Her right hand twitches for about a minute, and she watches it happen and tells me about it.” → **Focal aware**

**Rationale**

Accurate observation and description help the provider classify seizures and guide treatment and safety teaching. Seizures are **focal** (starting in one area of the brain) or **generalized** (involving both sides from the start).

**Focal aware:** the person stays aware. It may be a sensation (a rising feeling in the stomach, an unusual smell) or movement of one part of the body. A focal aware seizure that comes before a larger seizure is often called an **aura**, and it is documented.

**Focal impaired awareness:** the person is confused or unaware, often with staring and automatisms (lip smacking, fumbling), and does not remember it afterwards. A focal seizure can spread to both sides and become a tonic-clonic seizure.

**Generalized tonic-clonic:** stiffening (tonic phase) and a fall, then rhythmic jerking (clonic phase), often with tongue biting and incontinence, followed by a **postictal** period of confusion, headache, sore muscles and sleepiness.

**Absence:** brief (seconds) staring spells, often many times a day, with immediate recovery; most common in children.

**Phases:** prodrome (warning signs hours before, such as irritability), ictal (the seizure itself) and postictal (recovery).

### Screen 2: Analyze (matrix_mc)

*The nurse has reviewed the Diagnostic Results.*

The daughter asks whether her mother has dementia or Parkinson disease. For each finding, click to specify whether it is most consistent with **normal pressure hydrocephalus**, **Alzheimer disease**, or **Parkinson disease**.

- Walking improves after some CSF is removed by lumbar puncture → **Normal pressure hydrocephalus**
- Memory loss is the first problem; walking stays normal until late in the disease → **Alzheimer disease**
- Resting tremor, rigidity, and slow movement → **Parkinson disease**
- Wide-based, “magnetic” gait with urinary incontinence early in the illness → **Normal pressure hydrocephalus**

**Rationale**

**Normal pressure hydrocephalus (NPH):** CSF builds up in the ventricles because it is not absorbed well, but the pressure measured by lumbar puncture is normal or only slightly raised. The enlarged ventricles stretch the nerve fibres that run past them to the legs and bladder and frontal lobes, causing the gait, bladder and thinking changes. **Types of hydrocephalus:** **non-communicating (obstructive)**, when CSF flow is blocked inside the ventricular system (for example, a narrowed cerebral aqueduct or a tumour); **communicating (non-obstructive)**, when CSF flows freely but is not absorbed by the arachnoid villi (for example, after meningitis or a subarachnoid hemorrhage); and **normal pressure hydrocephalus**, a form of communicating hydrocephalus in older adults.
