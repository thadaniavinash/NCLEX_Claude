## NURS 1017 Unit 6 Case Study 1

54-year-old female client with symmetric hand and wrist synovitis diagnosed as seropositive rheumatoid arthritis (with osteoarthritis of the right knee), compared with osteoarthritis and osteomyelitis; methotrexate teaching, methotrexate toxicity with febrile neutropenia after daily dosing, and evaluation at 6 months including osteoporosis.

**Points to review:**

- Screen 2: anti-CCP has no MCC reference range; the range used (< 20 U/mL) varies by laboratory.
- Screen 4: folic acid 1 mg daily is one common regimen (5 mg once weekly is also used in Canada); adjust to your course's reference.
- Screen 5: leucovorin dose/route and the antibiotic are examples; the key does not depend on them.
- Screen 6: osteoporosis after about 10 weeks of prednisone is attributed to several risk factors together; check the wording suits your teaching.

### Screen 1: Recognize cues (select_all)

The nurse reviews the client's history and assessment. Which findings should the nurse recognize as cues of an **inflammatory (autoimmune) arthritis**? **Select all that apply.**

1. ✘ Crepitus in the right knee, which aches after long walks
2. ✔ Morning stiffness lasting about 2 hours
3. ✘ Blood pressure 138/84 mm Hg
4. ✔ Swelling, warmth, and tenderness of the MCP and PIP joints of both hands and both wrists
5. ✘ DIP joints not swollen
6. ✔ Fatigue, temperature 37.8° C, and an unintended 3-kg weight loss
7. ✘ Mother had arthritis of the knees in her 70s
8. ✔ Joints feel boggy (spongy) on palpation
9. ✔ Firm, nontender subcutaneous nodule over the right olecranon

**Rationale:**

**Cues of inflammatory (autoimmune) arthritis:**

**Prolonged morning stiffness:** stiffness lasting more than 1 hour after rest is typical of inflammatory arthritis. Fluid and inflammatory mediators build up in the inflamed synovium overnight. In osteoarthritis, stiffness usually lasts less than 30 minutes.

**Symmetric small-joint swelling:** rheumatoid arthritis (RA) classically involves the same small joints on both sides (MCP, PIP, wrists). Warmth and tenderness show active synovitis.

**Boggy joints:** a soft, spongy feel comes from thickened, inflamed synovium and joint fluid. Osteoarthritis joints feel hard and bony.

**Systemic signs:** fatigue, low-grade fever and weight loss reflect circulating inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6). RA is a systemic disease, not only a joint disease.

**Rheumatoid nodule:** a firm, nontender nodule over a pressure point such as the olecranon is an extra-articular feature of RA, seen mostly in seropositive disease.

**Not cues:** crepitus and pain after activity in one weight-bearing joint (the right knee) after an old injury point to osteoarthritis. The mother's knee arthritis in her 70s also sounds like osteoarthritis. Sparing of the DIP joints is expected in RA and is not a sign of disease. The blood pressure reflects her treated hypertension.

### Screen 2: Analyze cues (matrix_mr)

*The nurse has reviewed the Nurses' Notes from Day 3 1400, the Laboratory Results and the Diagnostic Results.*

The nurse prepares to answer the client's question about how her condition differs from other bone and joint disorders. For each finding, click to specify whether it is consistent with **rheumatoid arthritis**, **osteoarthritis**, or **osteomyelitis**. Each finding may support more than one condition.

- Morning stiffness lasting longer than 1 hour → **Rheumatoid arthritis**
- Stiffness lasting less than 30 minutes after rest; pain worse with use and relieved by rest → **Osteoarthritis**
- Symmetric swelling of the MCP and PIP joints of both hands → **Rheumatoid arthritis**
- Hard, bony enlargement of the DIP joints (Heberden nodes) → **Osteoarthritis**
- Positive rheumatoid factor and anti-CCP antibodies → **Rheumatoid arthritis**
- Elevated ESR and C-reactive protein → **Rheumatoid arthritis, Osteomyelitis**
- Elevated WBC count and a bone culture growing Staphylococcus aureus → **Osteomyelitis**
- Joint-space narrowing on X-ray → **Rheumatoid arthritis, Osteoarthritis**
- Osteophytes (bone spurs) and subchondral sclerosis on X-ray → **Osteoarthritis**
- Sequestrum (dead bone) on imaging and a draining sinus tract → **Osteomyelitis**

**Rationale:**

**Rheumatoid arthritis (RA)** is a systemic autoimmune disease of the synovium. Long morning stiffness (over 1 hour), symmetric swelling of small joints (MCP, PIP, wrists), positive rheumatoid factor and especially **anti-CCP antibodies** (highly specific for RA) are typical. Inflammation raises the **ESR and CRP**. X-rays show periarticular osteopenia, uniform **joint-space narrowing** and marginal erosions (as in this client's hands).

**Osteoarthritis (OA)** is a degenerative disease of articular cartilage, not an autoimmune one. Stiffness after rest is brief (under 30 minutes), and pain worsens with use and eases with rest. Bony enlargement of the DIP joints (**Heberden nodes**) and PIP joints (Bouchard nodes) is typical. ESR and CRP are usually normal and serology is negative. X-rays show uneven **joint-space narrowing**, **osteophytes** and subchondral sclerosis (as in this client's right knee).

**Osteomyelitis** is an infection of bone, most often by *Staphylococcus aureus*, spread through the blood or from an open fracture, surgery or a nearby wound. Infection causes fever, an elevated **WBC count**, and raised **ESR and CRP** (used to follow the response to antibiotics). A bone biopsy and culture identify the organism. In chronic osteomyelitis, dead bone (**sequestrum**) is walled off by new bone (involucrum), and pus may drain through a **sinus tract** to the skin.

**Shared findings:** ESR and CRP rise with inflammation from any cause (RA and osteomyelitis). Joint-space narrowing occurs in both RA and OA, because both destroy articular cartilage, by different mechanisms.

**Rationale:**

**Rheumatoid arthritis of the hands and wrists:** symmetric synovitis of the MCP and PIP joints and wrists, a strongly positive anti-CCP (210 U/mL) and rheumatoid factor, raised ESR and CRP, anemia of chronic inflammation (Hgb 108 g/L), and marginal erosions on X-ray. Smoking is a risk factor for RA but is not evidence that she has it. A normal uric acid argues against gout but does not show RA. Osteophytes and subchondral sclerosis are OA findings.

**Osteoarthritis of the right knee:** one weight-bearing joint, a previous injury, pain after use, crepitus without warmth or effusion, and osteophytes with subchondral sclerosis on X-ray. A client can have both diseases; her mother's knee arthritis was most likely OA too, which answers her question.

The synovium thickens into **pannus**, granulation tissue that releases enzymes and activates osteoclasts, eroding cartilage and the bone at the joint margins. Without early treatment this leads to permanent deformities (ulnar deviation, swan-neck and boutonnière deformities) and loss of hand function, which is why disease-modifying treatment is started promptly.

**Why not the others:** mechanical wear with bone spurs is OA; urate crystals cause gout; bacterial infection of bone is osteomyelitis.

### Screen 4: Generate solutions (select_all)

*The nurse has reviewed the Nurses' Notes from Day 3 1430 and the Orders.*

The nurse plans teaching for the client. Which instructions should the nurse include? **Select all that apply.**

1. ✘ Rest the inflamed joints in a flexed position supported by pillows
2. ✔ Take methotrexate once a week on the same day, using a calendar or pill organizer
3. ✘ Expect methotrexate to relieve the joint pain within 2 to 3 days
4. ✔ Avoid alcohol while taking methotrexate
5. ✔ Report fever, sore throat, mouth sores, unusual bruising, or a new dry cough right away
6. ✘ Stop taking prednisone as soon as the joints feel better
7. ✔ Take a warm shower or use warm, moist heat in the morning to ease stiffness
8. ✘ Live vaccines are safe to receive while taking methotrexate
9. ✔ Use large joints and assistive devices to protect the small joints of the hands, and wear the resting splints at night
10. ✔ Stopping smoking can reduce RA activity and improve the response to treatment

**Rationale:**

**Weekly methotrexate:** for RA, methotrexate is taken **once a week**. Taking it daily by mistake is a well-known, sometimes fatal error, so a fixed day and a calendar or pill organizer are taught from the start. Folic acid reduces mouth sores, nausea and liver enzyme rises.

**Alcohol:** methotrexate and alcohol are both toxic to the liver, so alcohol should be avoided (the client drinks most evenings).

**Warning signs:** methotrexate can suppress the bone marrow (infection, bleeding), cause mucositis, and rarely cause pneumonitis (dry cough, shortness of breath). These are reported promptly.

**Heat and joint protection:** warm, moist heat eases morning stiffness. Using larger joints (carrying a bag on the forearm), jar openers and built-up handles, and resting splints at night protect inflamed joints and keep them in a functional position. Balancing rest with gentle range-of-motion exercise maintains function.

**Smoking:** smoking increases RA activity and the production of anti-CCP antibodies, and lowers the response to methotrexate. It is also a risk factor for osteoporosis.

**Incorrect:** resting joints flexed on pillows leads to flexion contractures. Methotrexate takes about 6 to 12 weeks to work, which is why prednisone is used as a short bridge. Prednisone must be tapered as prescribed, never stopped suddenly (adrenal insufficiency). Live vaccines are avoided during immunosuppressive therapy; inactivated and recombinant vaccines (influenza, pneumococcal, recombinant zoster) are given.

### Screen 5: Take action (matrix_mc)

*The nurse has reviewed the Nurses' Notes from Day 26 0830, the Vital Signs and Laboratory Results from Day 26, and the Orders from Day 26.*

The client is admitted to the medical unit. For each nursing action, click to specify whether it is **indicated**, **contraindicated**, or **nonessential** for the client at this time.

- Measure the temperature rectally every 4 hours → **Contraindicated**
- Hold all further doses of methotrexate → **Indicated**
- Obtain the blood cultures before giving the first dose of piperacillin-tazobactam → **Indicated**
- Hold the prednisone until the infection has resolved → **Contraindicated**
- Administer leucovorin (folinic acid) as prescribed → **Indicated**
- Repeat the X-rays of both hands and wrists today → **Nonessential**

**Rationale:**

The client took methotrexate daily instead of weekly for 5 days and now has **methotrexate toxicity**: severe neutropenia (absolute neutrophils 0.4 × 10⁹/L), thrombocytopenia (74 × 10⁹/L), mucositis, raised ALT, and fever of 38.6° C with tachycardia (**febrile neutropenia**, a medical emergency).

**Indicated:** stop the drug (hold methotrexate). Give **leucovorin**, the reduced folate that bypasses the enzyme methotrexate blocks, as the antidote. Draw **blood cultures before the first antibiotic dose** so the organism can be identified, then start the antibiotic without delay. A soft toothbrush and saline rinses reduce mucosal injury, bleeding and infection.

**Contraindicated:** rectal temperatures can tear the mucosa and introduce bacteria in a neutropenic client with low platelets. Stopping long-term prednisone suddenly can cause adrenal crisis, and illness raises steroid needs; the order is to continue it. Visitors with respiratory infections should not visit a severely neutropenic client, even with a mask.

**Nonessential:** repeat hand X-rays do not change the management of this emergency; joint damage is followed over months.

## NURS 1017 Unit 6 Case Study 2

22-year-old male client after a motorcycle collision with a closed comminuted femur fracture and an anterior shoulder dislocation (after earlier subluxations); fracture types, dislocation vs subluxation, fat embolism syndrome on Day 3, the five stages of bone healing, and delayed union at 6 months.

**Points to review:**

- Screen 5: the five stages of bone healing are worded hematoma, cellular proliferation, callus, ossification, remodeling; match your textbook's names.
- Screen 6: the NSAID effect on bone healing is described as possible (the evidence is mixed); timing of delayed union for the femur (no bridging at 6 months) should suit your teaching.
- Screen 3: timing of fat embolism (about 36 hours after injury) and the ABG values are illustrative.

### Screen 1: Recognize cues (highlight)

The nurse reviews the Nurses' Notes. **Highlight** the findings that require immediate follow-up.

- ✔ Right thigh swollen and deformed, with the right leg shortened and externally rotated.
- ✘ Skin over the right thigh intact.
- ✘ Right foot warm and pink; pedal pulses 2+; capillary refill 2 seconds.
- ✔ Left shoulder has a squared-off contour; holds the left arm slightly away from his body and cannot move it.
- ✔ Reports numbness over the outer aspect of the left upper arm.
- ✘ Left radial pulse 2+.
- ✘ Abrasions on the left forearm and left knee.
- ✔ Skin pale, cool, and diaphoretic.
- ✘ T 36.6° C
- ✔ P 116 beats/min
- ✘ RR 22 breaths/min
- ✔ BP 100/62 mm Hg
- ✘ SpO₂ 97% on room air

**Rationale:**

**Require immediate follow-up:**

**Shortened, externally rotated, deformed right leg:** the classic picture of a displaced femoral shaft fracture. The limb must be immobilized (splinted or in traction) before any movement, and neurovascular status is checked often.

**Tachycardia (116 beats/min), BP 100/62 mm Hg, and pale, cool, diaphoretic skin:** a closed femoral shaft fracture can hide 1 to 1.5 L of blood in the thigh. These are early signs of hypovolemic shock and must be reported now.

**Squared-off shoulder that he cannot move:** the humeral head has left the joint (anterior dislocation is the most common type). A dislocation is reduced as soon as possible to protect the nerves, blood vessels and joint cartilage.

**Numbness over the outer upper arm:** the axillary nerve wraps around the neck of the humerus and is the nerve most often injured by an anterior shoulder dislocation. Sensation is documented before and after reduction.

**Do not need immediate follow-up:** intact skin over the fracture (a closed fracture), a warm, pink foot with strong pulses and brisk capillary refill, a strong left radial pulse, a temperature of 36.6° C, SpO₂ 97% and an RR of 22 breaths/min (expected with pain). The abrasions need cleaning but are not urgent.

### Screen 2: Analyze cues (matrix_mc)

*The nurse has reviewed the Nurses' Notes from Day 1 1620 and the Diagnostic Results.*

For each finding, click to specify whether it is consistent with a **fracture**, a **dislocation**, or a **subluxation**.

- Humeral head lying completely outside the glenoid cavity on X-ray → **Dislocation**
- Shortening and external rotation of the leg → **Fracture**
- Shoulder that “popped partly out” and slid back in on its own → **Subluxation**
- Bone broken into several fragments on X-ray → **Fracture**
- Partial loss of contact between the joint surfaces, which remain partly aligned → **Subluxation**
- Squared-off shoulder contour with loss of normal movement → **Dislocation**

**Rationale:**

**Fracture:** a break in the continuity of a bone. This client's femur fracture is **closed** (skin intact) and **comminuted** (more than two fragments). Other types: **open** (bone ends break through the skin), **transverse** (straight across), **oblique** (at an angle), **spiral** (twisting around the shaft from a rotational force), **greenstick** (incomplete, on one side of a bendable child's bone), **compression** (bone crushed, as in vertebrae), **impacted** (one fragment driven into another), **avulsion** (a fragment pulled off by a tendon or ligament) and **pathologic** (through bone weakened by disease). Muscle spasm pulls the overriding femur fragments together, shortening and rotating the leg.

**Dislocation:** the joint surfaces lose contact **completely**, as the X-ray shows for the left shoulder. The joint looks deformed (the shoulder is squared off because the humeral head no longer fills out the deltoid) and cannot move normally.

**Subluxation:** a **partial** dislocation; the joint surfaces still touch in part. It often reduces on its own, as in his hockey injuries. Repeated subluxations stretch the capsule and ligaments, which made this full dislocation more likely.

His blood pressure is stable, so this is not hypovolemic shock; some fall in hemoglobin after surgery is expected. Alcohol withdrawal can cause confusion but not hypoxemia and petechiae. Fever and the hemoglobin value alone do not point to a cause.

**Priority:** airway and breathing come first. The client's oxygenation is failing (impaired gas exchange), which also explains his confusion. Pain, infection risk and mobility matter but are not life-threatening now.

### Screen 5: Take action (ordered_response)

*The nurse has reviewed the Nurses' Notes from Day 6 1000 and the Vital Signs from Day 6 1000.*

The nurse uses the client's question to teach him how a fracture heals. Place the stages of bone healing in order, from the first stage to the last.

1. Hematoma formation: bleeding at the fracture forms a clot between the bone ends, and inflammation begins
2. Cellular proliferation: new capillaries, fibroblasts and bone-forming cells grow into the clot, forming granulation tissue
3. Callus formation: cartilage and immature (woven) bone form a soft callus that bridges the fracture
4. Ossification: the callus is mineralized into hard bone, uniting the fragments
5. Remodeling: osteoclasts remove excess callus and the bone regains its shape and strength

**Rationale:**

**1. Hematoma formation (first days):** torn blood vessels bleed into the fracture site and a clot forms. Inflammatory cells clear debris, and the clot becomes the framework for repair.

**2. Cellular proliferation (about days 3 to 14):** capillaries, fibroblasts and osteoblasts invade the clot, turning it into granulation tissue.

**3. Callus formation (weeks 2 to 6):** cartilage and woven bone form a callus around and between the bone ends. The fracture is held together but is not yet strong, which is why weight bearing is limited.

**4. Ossification (weeks 3 to 12 or more):** minerals are laid down and the callus becomes hard bone. On X-ray, bridging bone crosses the fracture line.

**5. Remodeling (months to a year or more):** osteoclasts remove the excess callus and the bone is reshaped along the lines of stress, restoring near-normal strength.

Healing times vary with the bone, the age of the client, the blood supply and other factors. A large weight-bearing bone such as the femur typically takes several months to unite. Smoking, poor nutrition, low vitamin D, infection, diabetes, corticosteroids and movement at the fracture site all slow healing.

## NURS 1017 Unit 6 Case Study 3

61-year-old female client with type 2 diabetes and peripheral neuropathy whose plantar ulcer spreads to the bone of the great toe (contiguous osteomyelitis); risk factors and local/systemic signs, sequestrum and involucrum, sepsis risk, home IV therapy via a PICC and evaluation after 6 weeks.
